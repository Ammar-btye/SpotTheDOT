## 3. Patterns
**What separated agent projects that won overall from the rest of the field**
1. **The agent takes real actions that judges can watch.** Alto opens a fix, posts to Slack and redeploys. Bye! Buy! actually lists, negotiates and collects payment. Voider pays through Visa. Mobius shipped a deployed SaaS and published 11 hours of logs. FaceTimeOS (logged elsewhere) won 1st at Cal Hacks by sending a screenshot of the finished task back to the caller. In each case the action is the climax of the demo, not a slide.
2. **Reliability and evaluation work is a new route to the top prizes.** All three LA Hacks 2026 podium spots involved agents, and two were *about* agents: codebreaker built its own benchmark, and Autopsy learns from agent failures. MultiEval (Wilson CIs, trace viewer), the Hack the North Cua winner (retry and safety logic, HUD evals) and HackOverflow and AgentOverflow (sandbox-verified fixes) all sell trust rather than one more capability. Expect judges, especially Cognition, Elastic and YC, to reward agents that measure themselves.
3. **A new input channel or a physical body beats a chat box.** Winners reached the user through FaceTime and iMessage (FaceTimeOS), text messages (Poke SDR), voice (Bloom, Voider, VisionOS, Tango, Jarvis), smart glasses (Lumos), the clipboard (Tango), biometrics (Motherly.ai) and a rover (RIIS). The plain computer-use agent Jarvis won only an MLH prize. FaceTimeOS, with the same idea but a novel channel, won 1st Overall.
4. **A specific, sympathetic user in a high-stakes domain.** Examples: 911 operators (Dispatch), ER staff (EDFlow), wildfire commanders (ContainOS), blind coders (VisionOS), seniors (Bloom), Alzheimer's patients (Lumos), disaster survivors (RIIS) and renewable-energy siting (TerraLink). Generic "assistant" pitches win sponsor prizes at most.
5. **Multi-agent orchestration is now expected, so name the agents.** Winners describe named roles: Alto's Extractor, Triage and Hypothesis agents; RIIS's Scout, Triage and Comms agents; TerraLink's four-agent pipeline; EDFlow's per-department specialists. Naming the roles makes the architecture legible in a 3-minute demo.
6. **Stacking sponsor prizes is easy with agent tooling.** Fetch.ai (uAgents, Agentverse, ASI:One) sponsored tracks at Cal Hacks, TreeHacks, LA Hacks and MHacks, and many overall winners also took the Fetch track (Alto, Lumos, RIIS). Browserbase/Stagehand, Elastic Agent Builder, Letta, Lava, Cua and Modal each pay for one integration. Claude is the most common "Built With" model among the action-taking winners, with Gemini close behind.
7. **"Cursor for X" and "Stack Overflow for agents" are recurring pitch frames**: Lavoe, Robosurge (logged elsewhere), AgentOverflow and HackOverflow. A familiar frame anchors judges instantly, though the second version of the same frame at an event looks derivative.

**Takeaways for our Knight Hacks project**
1. Build the demo around **one irreversible-looking real action**, such as a purchase made, a PR opened, a form filed or a call placed. Show it live, then show the receipt or screenshot.
2. Add a **reliability layer judges can see**: a mini-eval with a success rate over N runs, a trace viewer, or a verify-then-act step. It sets us apart from the many plain "agent that does X" entries.
3. Choose a **niche, sympathetic Florida-relevant user** (hurricane responders, elder care, students, 911 or EMS dispatch) and reach them through a **non-chat channel** (phone, SMS, voice or glasses).
4. Architect it as **2–4 named agents** and deliberately pick sponsor tools so the core build qualifies for the main track plus 2–3 sponsor prizes (an MCP tool layer and browser automation are cheap add-ons).
5. Keep the scope tight enough that the agent works end to end, live, and fast enough for a 3-minute demo, with a recorded fallback.
